\begin{abstract}
After a hurricane makes landfall, relief organizations face a
critical allocation problem: which communities need food
assistance most urgently? Damage assessments arrive days after
the event, too late to pre-position resources. We present a
ZIP-code-level framework that fuses eight administrative,
demographic, and geospatial data sources to prioritize food
relief before post-event inspection data becomes available. Our
analytic base table spans 14 Atlantic hurricanes (2016--2024),
yielding 5,522 ZIP-code$\times$hurricane observations across six
states. A multi-class XGBoost classifier predicts housing-damage
severity (\textit{Low\,/\,Medium\,/\,High\,/\,Severe}, measured by
FEMA inspections per resident)
from community vulnerability, storm-track geometry, and
food-access indicators, excluding post-event FEMA inspection
counts from the inputs. On five held-out storms (Ida, Ian,
Idalia, Helene, Milton; 2021--2024) it reaches a macro ROC-AUC
of 0.78 and a Severe-class recall of 0.80; leave-one-hurricane-out
evaluation over all 14 storms gives $0.76\pm0.08$, exposing
substantial storm-to-storm variation. Isotonic-calibrated risk is
combined with a food-fragility composite---food-desert flags,
SNAP retailer density, supermarket proximity, and the CDC Social
Vulnerability Index---into a \textit{Food Relief Priority Index}
(FRPI). Within each held-out storm, 92\% of the FRPI top decile
are ZIP codes whose FEMA inspection rate was later High or Severe
(per-storm base rate 50\%), and the FRPI's rank correlation with
applicant-reported food need is 0.67, though that count largely
mirrors damage. Because the model's skill depends on knowing the storm track,
we stress-test it with National Hurricane Center-scale forecast
errors: macro ROC-AUC falls to 0.73 at 24\,h and 0.64 at
48--72\,h lead time. A Fairlearn audit finds Severe-class recall
rising from 0.63 in the least to 0.87 in the most socially
vulnerable quartile; we discuss why this alone does not establish
equitable allocation. A Streamlit dashboard with per-ZIP SHAP
explanations supports human-in-the-loop decisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Tropical cyclones are the costliest U.S. weather disasters,
accounting for more than half of all billion-dollar disaster
losses since 1980~\cite{noaa_billion_dollar}. Beyond physical destruction,
hurricanes trigger acute food-access crises: supply chains
collapse, grocery stores lose power, and food-insecure households
face simultaneous income loss and resource scarcity. Even before a
major storm, an estimated 12.8\% of U.S. households experienced
food insecurity in 2022~\cite{coleman_jensen_food}, and studies
after Hurricanes Harvey and Ida document post-storm food
insecurity and uneven access to emergency food~\cite{garney_harvey_food, broyles_ida_food}.
Federal and non-governmental relief organizations must rapidly
allocate food and nutrition resources across hundreds of affected
ZIP codes, yet FEMA inspection data arrive only days to weeks after
landfall~\cite{fema_response_timeline}.

This delay creates a critical operational gap: relief
organizations must decide \textit{where to send resources first}
without reliable damage data. Current practice relies on
categorical storm-track metrics (wind speed, category) and
historical precedent, neither of which accounts for community
food vulnerability or demographic risk. High-wind ZIP codes with
abundant food infrastructure and low poverty rates need
fundamentally different interventions than equally-damaged
ZIP codes that are food deserts.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Prior work on disaster response optimization has addressed
logistics routing~\cite{logistics_routing}, shelter
allocation~\cite{shelter_allocation}, and social media-based
damage detection~\cite{imran_social_media}, but relatively few
studies have attempted to predict food-specific resource demand
at the sub-county level using pre-landfall community data.
Existing hurricane damage models largely predict continuous
economic loss rather than categorical relief priority
\cite{hurricane_damage_models}, and few incorporate food-access
indicators or formal equity constraints. Many evaluations also
use random row-level splits that mix observations from the same
event across training and test sets. We instead hold out entire
storms and report how performance varies across them.

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper makes four principal contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A storm-held-out evaluation of pre-landfall
    damage triage} --- a temporal split on five unseen storms,
    leave-one-hurricane-out and expanding-window evaluation over
    all 14 storms, a ZIP-disjoint control, and a stress test with
    NHC-scale track and intensity forecast errors that measures
    how much skill survives at realistic lead times.
    \item \textbf{Integration of food-fragility geography with
    disaster-risk modeling} --- a ZIP-code-level \textit{Food Relief
    Priority Index} (FRPI) that combines calibrated damage risk
    with food-access and social-vulnerability indicators,
    validated against withheld FEMA damage records and against
    applicant-reported food need.
    \item \textbf{A decision-support dashboard} --- a Streamlit
    interface with per-ZIP SHAP explanations, designed to inform
    rather than replace emergency managers' allocation decisions.
    \item \textbf{An equity audit with stated limits} ---
    Severe-class recall rises across social vulnerability
    quartiles (Q1: 0.63\,$\rightarrow$\,Q4: 0.87), but predicted
    Severe rates also outpace observed base rates, and FEMA
    labels may encode registration disparities; we report both.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows:
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work;
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the data sources, dataset
construction, and modeling pipeline; Section~\ref{sec:results}
presents results and evaluation; Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses deployment implications and limitations; and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine Learning for Disaster Response}
Machine learning has been applied to disaster response across
several domains. Imran~et~al.~\cite{imran_social_media} surveyed
social media processing for mass emergencies;
Castillo~et~al.~\cite{castillo_credibility} explored information
credibility on Twitter. Wang~et~al.
\cite{wang_disaster_logistics} studied facility-location
optimization under uncertainty, a building block of relief
pre-positioning, while Nateghi~et~al.
\cite{nateghi_power_outage} predicted hurricane-induced power
outages using tree-based ensemble models.
Nguyen~et~al.~\cite{nguyen_damage_imagery} applied convolutional
neural networks to social-media imagery for disaster damage
assessment, and Alam~et~al.~\cite{alam_crisismmd} released
CrisisMMD, a multimodal dataset of disaster-related tweets. Our work differs from all of the above in
targeting \textit{pre-landfall food-specific relief demand} using
administrative and demographic data rather than post-event
imagery or social media signals.

\subsection{Food Insecurity and Natural Disasters}
Chakraborty~et~al.~\cite{chakraborty_harvey} documented the
disproportionate impacts of Hurricane Harvey on socially
vulnerable populations. Clay~et~al.~\cite{clay_katrina_food} found that 23\% of
households heavily affected by Hurricane Katrina remained food
insecure five years later, with social support and self-efficacy
protective.
The USDA Food Access Research Atlas~\cite{usda_food_atlas}
provides the community-level food-access indicators our model
incorporates.

\subsection{Vulnerability and Equity in Disaster ML}
The CDC Social Vulnerability Index (SVI)~\cite{flanagan_svi} has
been widely used to identify communities at heightened disaster
risk. Cutter~et~al.~\cite{cutter_svi} established the social
vulnerability framework that informs our feature design.
Mehrabi~et~al.~\cite{mehrabi_fairness} surveyed bias and
fairness in ML systems; our equity audit uses the Fairlearn
toolkit of Bird~et~al.~\cite{bird_fairlearn}, which implements
the demographic parity ratio and equalized odds difference.

\subsection{Probabilistic Calibration in ML Systems}
Guo~et~al.~\cite{guo_calibration} showed that modern classifiers
are often over-confident and compared post-hoc corrections
including Platt scaling, temperature scaling, and isotonic
regression. Because the FRPI consumes class probabilities
directly, we apply isotonic calibration before computing the
index (Section~\ref{sec:clf_results}).

\subsection{Explainable AI for Humanitarian Systems}
SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)~\cite{lundberg_shap}
provides model-agnostic feature attribution that is useful for
operational trust in humanitarian settings. We employ TreeSHAP
for the XGBoost classifier to generate per-ZIP explanations in
the deployment dashboard. Arrieta~et~al.~\cite{arrieta_xai}
surveyed explainable AI for responsible deployment in
high-stakes settings.

To our knowledge, no prior work combines pre-landfall ML-based
damage prediction with food-access geography, a composite relief
priority index, a formal equity audit, and probabilistic
calibration analysis at ZIP-code granularity across multiple
hurricane seasons.

\section{Data and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data Sources}
Our analytic base table integrates eight data sources at
ZIP-code granularity. They arrive at five different spatial units
(census tract, ZCTA, ZIP code, retailer point, and storm-track
point) and several release years, and together summarize 2.95
million FEMA housing inspections; the food-need validation
(Section~\ref{sec:frpi_val}) adds 2.04 million individual FEMA
registrations. Reconciling these units and vintages, rather than
the model itself, is a large part of the engineering effort:

\textbf{1. FEMA Housing Assistance (HA) v2 API:}
Owners and Renters Housing Assistance records providing FEMA
inspection counts and approved IHP dollar amounts
per ZIP code and disaster number. Owners and Renters tables use
asymmetric damage schemas: owner records use dollar-range buckets
(\$0, \$1--10k, \$10k--20k, \$20k--30k, \textgreater\$30k) while
renter records use categorical labels (no damage, moderate, major,
substantial). We reconcile these by mapping owner \textgreater\$20k
and renter major+substantial to a unified \texttt{total\_major\_substantial}
count, then sum owner and renter populations per (disaster, ZIP)
to prevent Cartesian explosion when a ZIP spans multiple counties.

\textbf{2. Census ACS 5-Year (2021):}
Twenty variables queried at the ZCTA level via the Census API,
including total population (B01003), median household income
(B19013), poverty count (B17001), renter-occupied units (B25003),
age-disaggregated elderly counts, racial composition, no-vehicle
households (B08201), and mobile homes (B25024).

\textbf{3. CDC Social Vulnerability Index 2022:}
Four SVI theme scores (socioeconomic status, household
composition and disability, minority status and language,
housing type and transportation) plus the composite SVI overall
score, aggregated from census-tract to ZIP via HUD USPS crosswalk
using residential-ratio weighted averaging.

\textbf{4. USDA Food Access Research Atlas (2019):}
Tract-level LILA (Low-Income, Low-Access) flags, low-access
low-income population counts, and the number of housing units
receiving SNAP (\texttt{TractSNAP}), aggregated to ZIP via the
same HUD crosswalk. A binary \texttt{food\_desert\_flag} is
assigned when the tract-weighted LILA share $\geq 0.5$.

\textbf{5. USDA SNAP Retailer Historical Locator:}
Authorized SNAP retailer point locations spatially joined to ZCTA
polygons (EPSG:4326) to compute per-ZIP retailer counts and
geodesic distance to the nearest supermarket (pyproj.Geod,
WGS84).

\textbf{6. FEMA National Risk Index (NRI, Census Tract):}
Coastal flood risk score (\texttt{CFLD\_RISKS}) and hurricane
hazard risk score (\texttt{HRCN\_RISKS}) aggregated from census
tracts to ZIP via the HUD crosswalk. These scores supplement an
NFHL floodplain overlay (\texttt{pct\_in\_100yr\_floodplain}) that
is available for only 2\% of rows.

\textbf{7. NOAA IBTrACS v04r01~\cite{knapp_ibtracs}:}
Storm track coordinates for each hurricane. Geodesic distance
from each ZIP centroid (projected in EPSG:5070 before centroid
computation) to the nearest point on the storm track is computed
via \texttt{pyproj.Geod.inv}, never Euclidean distance.

\textbf{8. HUD USPS TRACT-ZIP Crosswalk:}
Residential-ratio weighted crosswalk used to aggregate all
tract-level data sources to ZIP codes, preserving the share of
residential addresses in each tract.

\textbf{Temporal resolution of storm features.}
\texttt{distance\_to\_track\_km} is ZIP-specific but is measured
to the \textit{observed} best track, and \texttt{max\_wind\_speed\_kt}
and \texttt{hurricane\_category} are storm-level constants (the
storm's lifetime peak), not ZIP-level winds. All three are
therefore known exactly only in hindsight. Every other feature
is static and available before the storm season.
Section~\ref{sec:robust} replaces the storm features with
forecast-quality values to measure what survives before landfall.

\subsection{Dataset Construction}

\textbf{Hurricane Scope and Temporal Split:}
We include 14 Atlantic hurricanes from 2016 to 2024 that
activated FEMA Individual Assistance programs with
ZIP-code-level housing assistance records (Table~\ref{tab:hurricanes}).
Nicholas (2021) and Dorian (2019) are excluded due to absence
of FEMA IA records. The split is strictly hurricane-level:
TRAIN (2016--2018, 5 storms), VAL (2020, 4 storms), and TEST
(2021--2024, 5 storms). Each storm is represented by its primary
state's FEMA Individual Assistance declaration (e.g., Helene:
Georgia; Ida: Louisiana), so multi-state storms are covered only
in that state. Storms, not ZIP codes, are held out:
80\% of TEST rows are ZIP codes that also appear in an earlier
storm. Section~\ref{sec:robust} shows
that removing these ZIPs from training leaves results unchanged.

\textbf{Target Variable:}
The primary classification target, \texttt{damage\_severity\_class},
is derived from the rate \texttt{verified\_damage\_per\_1000}, computed
as \texttt{total\_inspected} / \texttt{population} $\times$ 1000. This rate is binned into four
ordered classes: \textit{Low} ($<2$), \textit{Medium}
($2 \leq x < 8$), \textit{High} ($8 \leq x < 25$), and
\textit{Severe} ($\geq 25$) inspected households per 1,000
residents. \textit{Severe} (at least one inspected household per
40 residents) marks widespread damage, whereas \textit{High} (one
per 40--125) marks substantial but less widespread damage. We model severity as ordered \textit{classes} rather
than a continuous rate because relief triage is a tiered
decision, the rate is extremely right-skewed, and FEMA counts
are noisy at the low end; Section~\ref{sec:clf_results} compares
this choice with ordinal and regression formulations. The target
counts households FEMA inspected after registering for assistance,
so it is a proxy for housing damage, not a direct measure of damage
or of food need; Section~\ref{sec:frpi_val} tests the index against
reported food need. Throughout, ``damage'' refers to this rate.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}

Twenty-two continuous features, one binary feature, and two
categorical features are used in the model (Table~\ref{tab:features}).
All demographic percentage shares (e.g., \texttt{pct\_poverty},
\texttt{pct\_renters}) are derived from raw ACS counts and use
total population as the denominator (e.g., renter-occupied units
per resident).
CDC SVI missing-data sentinels ($-999$) are replaced by
state-level medians; flood and NRI columns are zero-filled (no
record implies no mapped hazard). \texttt{snap\_participation\_pct},
the tract-weighted \texttt{TractSNAP} count, is missing for 7\%
of rows and is median-imputed inside the model pipeline using
training-fold statistics only.

\subsection{Modeling Pipeline}

All models share a common \texttt{ColumnTransformer} preprocessor
implemented in scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa_sklearn}:
continuous features are median-imputed and scaled with
\texttt{RobustScaler}, categorical features are one-hot encoded
(drop-first), and the binary flag is passed through. The
\texttt{imblearn.Pipeline} applies SMOTE oversampling
\cite{chawla_smote} only to training data, never to held-out data.

Five classifiers are evaluated with fixed hyperparameters:
XGBoost \cite{chen_xgboost} (\texttt{multi:softprob}, 200 trees,
depth 6, $\eta=0.1$, subsample 0.8), Random Forest
(\texttt{class\_weight=balanced}, 200 trees), LinearSVC with Platt
calibration, Logistic Regression (SAGA, L2), and Gaussian Naive
Bayes. \textbf{Model selection} uses leave-one-storm-out
evaluation over the nine TRAIN+VAL storms, so the TEST storms
play no role in choosing the model. \textbf{Calibration:} the
selected classifier's probabilities are recalibrated with
isotonic regression fit on VAL; calibrated probabilities feed
the FRPI, while class predictions use the uncalibrated
\textit{argmax}.

\subsection{Food Relief Priority Index}

The \textit{Food Relief Priority Index} (FRPI) is a two-component
weighted score:
\begin{equation}
\text{FRPI} = 0.6 \cdot P(\text{High} \cup \text{Severe}) +
               0.4 \cdot \text{FFS}
\label{eq:frpi}
\end{equation}
where $P(\text{High} \cup \text{Severe})$ is the summed calibrated
probability of the two highest damage classes, and the Food
Fragility Score (FFS) averages the available components
$c \in \mathcal{C}$:
\begin{equation}
\text{FFS} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} c
\label{eq:ffs}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \{\hat{d},\, 1-\hat{s}_\text{count},\,
1-\hat{s}_\text{per1k},\, \hat{p}_\text{snap},\,
\hat{v}_\text{svi},\, f_\text{desert}\}$,
where $\hat{(\cdot)}$ denotes MinMax-scaled values in $[0,1]$,
$\hat{d}$ is distance to the nearest supermarket,
$\hat{s}_\text{count}$ and $\hat{s}_\text{per1k}$ are SNAP retailer
count and density (inverted), $\hat{p}_\text{snap}$ is
SNAP-recipient housing units, $\hat{v}_\text{svi}$ is SVI overall,
and $f_\text{desert}$ is the food desert flag. A component missing
for a ZIP is dropped from $\mathcal{C}$ rather than imputed. All
scalers are fit on TRAIN ZIPs only. Both FRPI terms lie in
$[0,1]$. The 0.6/0.4 weights favor the acute, storm-driven
signal over the chronic fragility baseline; they were set before
the TEST storms were evaluated and were not tuned on them.

Because the FFS reuses vulnerability indicators, high-FRPI ZIPs
are partly food deserts \textit{by construction}; the
food-desert share of top-ranked ZIPs is therefore a description
of the index, not evidence of its validity. Table~\ref{tab:frpi_sens}
shows how the weight trades damage capture for fragility
coverage, and Section~\ref{sec:frpi_val} validates the index
against outcomes it does not contain. With uncalibrated
probabilities, $P(\text{High}\cup\text{Severe})$ saturates near 1
for many ZIPs and the FFS alone decides the top ranks (100\%
food deserts at 0.6/0.4); calibration removes this artifact.

\section{Results and Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

Two limitations frame all results below. First, the storm
features are exact in our evaluation but must be forecast in
practice (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). Second, the exploratory
regression model fails on two TEST storms ($R^2<0$ for Ida and
Helene; Section~\ref{sec:regression}), so we use the ordered
classifier, not a continuous damage estimate, for ranking.

\subsection{Classification Performance on Held-Out Test Set}
\label{sec:clf_results}

Table~\ref{tab:clf_results} reports all five classifiers on the
TEST set (2,587 rows). TRAIN (2,323 rows) is smaller than TEST
because the 2021--2024 storms affected more ZIP codes; its class
distribution is Severe 51.9\% (1,205), Low 24.8\% (575), High
15.8\% (366), and Medium 7.6\% (177).
\textbf{Selection.} XGBoost has the highest leave-one-storm-out
macro ROC-AUC on the nine development storms (0.699 vs.\ 0.689 for
Random Forest) and is selected; it also has the highest TEST
ROC-AUC (0.784). Random Forest has higher TEST Severe recall
(0.825 vs.\ 0.797) and better-calibrated raw probabilities
(log-loss 1.07 vs.\ 1.42). After isotonic calibration on VAL,
XGBoost's Severe-class expected calibration error falls from
0.151 to 0.045 (Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}), its multiclass Brier score from 0.164 to 0.141
(uniform guessing: 0.188; TRAIN class priors: 0.204), and its

\textbf{Where the errors are.} Table~\ref{tab:perclass} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} show that overall accuracy (57\%) is
held down by the two middle classes. The largest error is
\textit{High predicted as Severe}: 323 of 541 High rows (60\%).
Severe precision is therefore 0.52, but 82\% of Severe
predictions are truly High \textit{or} Severe, and only 5\% are
truly Low. For allocation this boundary matters less than it
appears: the FRPI uses $P(\text{High}\cup\text{Severe})$, so
confusing the two upper classes barely changes a ZIP's rank
(Section~\ref{sec:frpi_val}). It does mean the model cannot
reliably separate ``substantial'' from ``extreme'' damage, so
quantities shipped to a ZIP should not be scaled by the predicted
class alone. Medium (F1 0.25) is the hardest class: it is the
rarest in TRAIN (7.6\%) and occupies a transitional damage regime.

\textbf{Why ordered classes.} We compared three formulations on
TEST: the multiclass model, an ordinal model (three cumulative
binary XGBoost models, $P(y>k)$~\cite{frank_hall_ordinal}), and an
XGBoost regressor on $\log(1+\text{rate})$ binned with the same
thresholds (the latter two without SMOTE). Their rank correlation with the damage rate is
similar ($\rho$ = 0.74, 0.73, 0.71), as is High$\cup$Severe
ROC-AUC (0.87, 0.88, 0.89), but the regressor recovers far fewer
Severe ZIPs (recall 0.66 vs.\ 0.80 multiclass and 0.82 ordinal),
because squared-error fitting shrinks predictions away from the
heavy upper tail. The ordinal model is a viable alternative; we
keep the multiclass model for its higher macro ROC-AUC (0.784
vs.\ 0.763).

\textbf{Storm-level uncertainty.} With only 14 independent
events, the single TEST split is optimistic: leave-one-hurricane-out
macro ROC-AUC averages $0.76\pm0.08$, and Severe recall varies
from 0.43 (Idalia) to 0.82 (Ida) among the TEST storms. Training
only on storms that occurred earlier gives similar results
($0.73\pm0.11$). Helene, whose Georgia ZIPs were severely damaged
far inland, is the hardest TEST storm under both schemes (ROC-AUC
0.63).

\textbf{ZIP overlap.} Because storms rather than ZIP codes are
held out, 2,059 of 2,587 TEST rows (80\%) are ZIPs that also
appear in TRAIN or VAL. Retraining after removing every TEST ZIP
from TRAIN gives the same macro ROC-AUC as a same-size random
subsample (0.778 vs.\ 0.779), and repeating each ZIP's most
recent observed class scores only 22\% accuracy, so the model is
not memorizing locations.

\textbf{Feature subsets.} Ten features (selected by SHAP on VAL)
recover most of the full model's performance (0.778 vs.\ 0.784).
Storm geometry alone reaches 0.705; static vulnerability and
hazard features add 0.08, and food-access features add nothing to
damage \textit{classification}---they matter only through the FFS.

\textbf{Data vintage.} Replacing the static 2021 ACS demographics
with each storm's own 5-year vintage (2016--2024; 99\% of rows
matched) leaves macro ROC-AUC unchanged (0.783 vs.\ 0.781 for the
same features rebuilt from the 2021 vintage) and High$\cup$Severe
ROC-AUC at 0.872 vs.\ 0.870. Severe recall falls from 0.78 to 0.73
while precision rises from 0.50 to 0.52, so the vintage mainly
shifts the Severe decision boundary rather than the model's
ranking. SVI (biennial, tract-level) and the 2019 Food Access
Research Atlas were not re-matched.

\textbf{Forecast error.} To mimic pre-landfall use, we displace
each TEST storm's entire track perpendicular to its heading by a
random offset whose mean magnitude equals the National Hurricane
Center's 2019--2023 mean official Atlantic track error (67, 116,
and 161\,km at 24, 48, and 72\,h~\cite{nhc_verification}), perturb
peak wind by the corresponding intensity error (7--11\,kt), and
recompute every ZIP's distance to the track (20 draws per lead
time). Skill degrades quickly (Fig.~\ref{fig:forecast}): macro ROC-AUC is 0.73 at 24\,h and
0.64 at 48--72\,h, against 0.56 with no storm information at all,
and only 58\% / 40\% of each storm's FRPI top decile is retained
at 24 / 48\,h. Training on similarly perturbed tracks stabilizes
the ranking (81\% retained at 48\,h) but caps ROC-AUC near 0.64
even with a perfect track. The framework is therefore most useful
inside roughly 24\,h of landfall, and rankings issued earlier
should be treated as provisional and refreshed with each NHC
advisory.

\texttt{nri\_hrcn\_score}, the category-4 indicator,
\texttt{svi\_socioeconomic}, \texttt{pct\_mobile\_homes},
\texttt{max\_wind\_speed\_kt}, \texttt{nri\_cflood\_score},

not reduce performance, so their attributions likely reflect
correlation with population and urbanization rather than an

\subsection{Priority Index Validation}
\label{sec:frpi_val}

We validate the FRPI against outcomes it does not contain,
within each TEST storm (averaged over the five storms).

\textbf{Damage capture and error propagation.} Within each storm,
91.6\% of the FRPI top decile are ZIP codes whose FEMA inspection rate was
High or Severe (storm base rate 49.8\%), and only 2.4\% are truly
Low, so the highest-ranked ZIPs are rarely false alarms. Misses are
the larger risk: only 55\% of truly Severe ZIP codes fall in their
storm's FRPI top quartile, so a coordinator serving only the top
quarter would miss nearly half of the most damaged areas. The
FRPI's rank correlation with the damage rate ($\rho=0.68$) is
essentially that of the damage-risk term alone (0.67), while the
FFS alone is nearly uncorrelated with damage ($\rho=0.14$); the
fragility term shifts priority toward food-fragile ZIPs with
somewhat lower damage (Table~\ref{tab:frpi_sens}) at little cost
in overall ranking quality.

\textbf{Independent food-need check.} FEMA registrations record
whether the applicant reported a need for food (2.04M such
registrations across the TEST storms). Food-need registrations per
1,000 residents rank-correlate with the FRPI at $\rho=0.67$ (top
decile: 2.9$\times$ the storm mean), slightly above the damage term
alone (0.66), but this count largely tracks damage ($\rho=0.94$).
The \textit{share} of registrants reporting food need, averaging
63\%, behaves differently: it rises with social vulnerability (SVI
$\rho=0.26$, FFS 0.20, positive in all five storms) and falls with
predicted damage ($-0.16$); the FRPI sits between ($-0.12$). The FFS
thus carries food-need information that damage risk lacks, but at
0.6/0.4 the FRPI ranks where food need is most \textit{abundant},
not where it is most \textit{prevalent} among affected households;
a higher fragility weight would shift this balance at some cost in
damage capture (Table~\ref{tab:frpi_sens}).

\textbf{IHP registration check.} FEMA inspection counts
and IHP valid registration counts are strongly correlated
(Pearson $r=0.91$ across all rows), so the target is not an
artifact of inspection scheduling, although both share the
registration bias discussed above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Operational Use}
The FRPI is intended as a decision-support input for
pre-positioning food once a storm's track is reasonably certain.
Our forecast-error analysis (Section~\ref{sec:robust}) indicates
that this is roughly the final 24\,h before landfall; at 48--72\,h,
rankings change substantially with each forecast and should be
treated as provisional. In the dashboard, a coordinator selects a
storm, sets a priority threshold, and receives a ranked ZIP list
with map overlays and per-ZIP SHAP attributions; in operational
use, rankings would be regenerated with each NHC advisory. The FRPI is a relative rank,
not a probability of need, and all outputs are advisory: no
resource dispatch is automated, and local knowledge (e.g., aid
already delivered by another agency) should override the ranking.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
Algorithmic priority systems in humanitarian settings carry
risks. Under time pressure, coordinators may treat the ranked
list as exhaustive; absence from the top-$N$ list is not evidence
of safety. The model learns from FEMA records that reflect who
applied for and received assistance, which may encode historical
disparities, so the equity audit should be repeated on each new
season's data. Community organizations and local governments in
affected areas should be consulted before operational use.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Storm inputs and out-of-range storms:} storm features are
exact only in hindsight, and static hazard scores do not
anticipate storms outside the training range, such as Ida's
extreme damage rates or Helene's inland damage.
\textbf{Few independent events:} 14 storms, with large
storm-to-storm variation (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).
\textbf{Proxy targets:} FEMA inspections measure housing damage,
not food need, and depend on registration; our food-need check
uses applicant self-reports, which share that registration bias.
\textbf{Data vintages:} SVI 2022 and the 2019 Food Access Research
Atlas are applied to all storms; year-matching the ACS demographics
did not change discrimination (Section~\ref{sec:robust}), but
conditions for older and newer storms may still differ.
\textbf{Aggregation:} ZIP codes are large and heterogeneous;
tract-level prediction would allow finer targeting.

\subsection{Future Work}
Future directions include: (1) adding forecast wind-field, rainfall, and
storm-surge inputs to capture ZIP-level hazard beyond track
distance; (2) driving the model with
NHC forecast-track ensembles rather than a single track, so that
rankings carry their own uncertainty; (3) validating against
direct food-assistance records (e.g., D-SNAP issuance) where
ZIP-level data can be obtained; and (4) extending to
tract-level prediction and non-hurricane hazards.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a ZIP-code-level food relief prioritization
framework evaluated on 14 Atlantic hurricanes. Holding out entire
storms, the XGBoost classifier reaches a macro ROC-AUC of 0.78 and
Severe-class recall of 0.80 on five unseen 2021--2024 storms,
with substantial variation across storms ($0.76\pm0.08$
leave-one-hurricane-out). Combined with a food-fragility score
into the FRPI, it places ZIP codes with High or Severe FEMA inspection rates in
92\% of each storm's top decile and ranks applicant-reported food
need about as well as damage ($\rho=0.67$), though it does not single
out communities where food need is most prevalent among affected
households. Two findings bound its use: its skill depends on knowing the storm
track, so it is most reliable within about 24\,h of landfall, and
it struggles on storms outside its training range, such as Ida and
Helene. The equity audit
shows the model does not under-detect damage in highly vulnerable
communities, but because FEMA records may themselves reflect
unequal registration, it does not establish equitable allocation.
We offer the FRPI as an interpretable, auditable input to human
decision-makers. Code, data pipeline, and dashboard are available
at \url{github.com/aneessaheba/hurricane-food-relief-ml}.
